# Rainkeep: game design and business plan

**Pitch:** The rain stopped a generation ago. Raise the last Rainwyrm, a water dragon whose cooling mist keeps a desert keep alive, and dig the wells that keep your people drinking. Rainkeep is a 3D survival city-builder with heroes, expeditions and alliances, aimed at the same audience as Whiteout Survival, with the survival game that its ads promise kept at the center of play.

This document covers what the game in this folder (version 4.3) contains, what makes it different enough to pull players away from Whiteout Survival, how it makes money, and what it would take to ship it.

---

## 1. Where Whiteout Survival is strong, and where it leaves an opening

Whiteout Survival (Century Games, 2023) is one of the highest-grossing mobile strategy games of the last few years; third-party trackers estimate its lifetime revenue well past $1B. We keep the structure that makes that work:

| Keep | Why it works |
|---|---|
| An apocalypse settlement built around a single life-giving source | Instantly readable premise, strong ad creative, a clear "keep them alive" fantasy |
| One central structure whose level gates every other building | Clear long-term goal; each upgrade unlocks visible growth |
| Collectible heroes with rarity, stars and gacha recruitment | The core of the revenue model in the genre |
| Short PvE stages early, alliances and server events later | Fast early wins, then social retention |
| Timers with speedups, battle pass, monthly card, packs | Proven spending ladder from $0.99 to whale tiers |

Complaints that come up again and again in Whiteout Survival's store reviews and community forums are where we win:

1. **The game in the ads isn't the game.** Ads show survivors freezing and heat management. In the real game that layer fades after the first hour and the game becomes a timer-and-power race.
2. **Late joiners and free players get crushed** by older servers and heavy spenders in server-versus-server events.
3. **Pack fatigue.** Constant pop-up offers and a feeling that every system has its own paywall.
4. **Weak attachment.** The furnace is a building; nobody loves a building.
5. **Sameness.** The frozen-apocalypse look has been copied by many games since; a new game in the same snow reads as a clone in an ad feed.

## 2. What makes Rainkeep different

These seven pillars are all in the game. Each one answers a complaint above.

1. **A new world: the desert that forgot the rain.** Sun-baked sandstone, palm groves, turquoise water, sandstorms and heatwaves. It is instantly distinguishable from the snow games in an ad feed, but the survival logic is just as primal: water is life. The setting also gives the game a natural day and night cycle, warm and cool palettes, and a hopeful ending (the rain coming back) that a frozen setting struggles to deliver.
2. **A living water source: the Rainwyrm.** Your "furnace" is a water dragon coiled in the keep's spring, rendered in real-time 3D: a sculpted head with golden slit eyes and a jaw that breathes the mist, ear frills, long whiskers, a scalloped fin sail down its back and iridescent scales. It grows through nine forms (Hatchling, Whelp, Drake, Tidewyrm, Elder, Ascended, Primordial, Stormcrowned, Skyriver), visibly gets bigger, its fins and frills widen and its horns lengthen and branch, trails a rain cloud of its own, wears a crown of storm horns with lightning in its cloud, and finally becomes a river that flies. It blinks, hums when petted, calls a torrent to open every battle, and goes dormant if the wells run dry. Players name it, and at Lv 12 they choose its Ascension (Monsoon, Mistveil or Floodheart). It gives players a pet to protect and a canvas for cosmetic spending (skins), revenue that doesn't add pay-to-win pressure.
3. **Survival that stays real all game.** Water has two jobs: survivors drink it and the wyrm breathes it as cooling mist. Weather is forecast ahead of time (Dust Haze, Sandstorm, Heatwave), and the sun "hunts water", so every time the wyrm grows, storms run hotter. Middays run hotter and desert nights run cold, so the right mist changes through the day. Players choose the mist (Drizzle, Steady, Downpour), trading water for cooling, and from Lv 6 can let an Attuned wyrm set it for them. From Lv 3 the wyrm can **Call the Rain**: a short shower on a long recharge that refills the wells, cools the keep and calms a sandstorm, so the big storm of the day becomes a decision about when to spend it. Heat makes survivors sick, the Healer's House treats them, and in a Scorching keep the sick can be lost. If the cisterns run dry, the wyrm sleeps and families leave to find water elsewhere.
4. **A real 3D keep and world.** The keep and the Dunes are 3D scenes, not static backdrops: buildings change shape as they level up, villagers carry water jars between the well and their work, camels circle the walls, palms sway harder in a sandstorm, lanterns come on at night, and caravans visibly cross the Dunes. The keep has real height: it is a terraced oasis at the head of a canyon, with the spring sunk in a stepped basin, the houses and barracks on raised terraces, an upper town and a temple carved into the cliff, so every building has a place rather than a slot on a ring. You can pan across it, zoom from the whole canyon down to street level and turn it. This is the screenshot and the ad. The interface stays out of its way: one header bar, three medallions on the side instead of a wall of buttons, and level stars instead of name plates, so the screen is mostly keep.
5. **Heroes matter at home, not just in fights.** Every hero can be stationed as a Steward of one building (Bashir boosts the wells, Halima speeds healing, Zahra cools the keep). Collecting heroes improves your keep as well as your squad, which deepens the gacha without adding more combat power. The cast is illustrated, diverse and original.
6. **Offline protection and fair competition.** While you're away, the wyrm keeps a gentle mist: production continues at a reduced rate and nobody gets sick. Oasis Wars, the leaderboard event, matches players into brackets by spending (Free, Supporter, Patron), so whales fight whales and newcomers fight newcomers. In 4.3 the nine rival keeps are simulated; in a live version they would be real players on bracketed servers.
7. **A story worth following.** Genre leaders tell their story in a paragraph per chapter. Rainkeep has three acts and three endings, a scene with a recurring cast before every boss, the last wyrm's sealed kin to bring home one by one (each visible on the canyon rim of your keep), and a three-chapter tale for every hero. Story is retention: a scene at the next boss and a tale chapter at the next hero level are reasons to come back that cost nothing to run.

### Naming and IP

Everything is original: the setting (the Long Noon, the Rainwyrm, the Sunheart), building names, hero names and bios, currencies (Starglass, Beacon Tokens, Field Journals), the 3D models (generated in code), the portraits and the icon. Heroes draw on many cultures without depicting any real people, places or religious sites. Before launch, run a trademark search on "Rainkeep" in the US, EU, UK, Japan and Korea. Do not use "Whiteout Survival" as a store keyword or in ad copy; Apple rejects competitor names in keywords and it invites a trademark claim. Comparative messaging ("tired of losing to whales?") is fine without naming them; have a lawyer review it.

## 3. Core loop

```
Minute to minute:  check forecast → set the mist → tap surplus bubbles → start an upgrade → send a caravan
Every few minutes: settle a keep incident → trade with a visiting merchant → Call the Rain before the storm
Session (5-10 min): claim quest → clear an expedition stage → level heroes → recruit → strike the Colossus
                   → climb a Mirage Spire floor → spend Duel tickets → forge the Warden's Gear
Day:               gift calendar, daily duties, patrol cache, Oasis Stipend, Caravan help and donations
Event (20 min*):   Rain Festival → Beast Hunt → Forge Festival → Builder's Rush → Spire Rush → Oasis Wars, on rotation
Duel season (3 h*): climb the ladder, earn Glory by rank, slip back and climb again
Season:            30-tier Wellkeeper's Ledger, new skins, more Dunes and Burning Line
```
*Game time. Timers and rates run about 30× faster than a typical live-service game.

**Pricing anchors in the game:** a single recruit costs 150 Starglass. The $9.99 pack buys about 4.7 recruits, and a 10-pull costs 1,350 Starglass, about $19. That is in line with genre norms; tune it in soft launch.

### Store and legal compliance (non-negotiable)

- **Apple in-app purchase** for all digital goods (App Store Review Guideline 3.1.1). The web build simulates purchases. The App Store build routes them through StoreKit via RevenueCat, which validates receipts server-side (see NATIVE.md).
- **Gacha odds disclosed before purchase.** Apple requires it for paid random items, and so do China, Korea and Japan. The game has an Odds sheet on the Beacon screen.
- **Paid random items are restricted in some countries** (Belgium bans them outright). Build a region switch that turns paid recruits into direct purchases or shard shops.
- **Age rating, parental controls and spending limits for minors**, plus clear refund flows. In-game currency prices must show real-money equivalents where local law requires.

## 5. How we pull Whiteout Survival players over

1. **Message:** "The survival game you were promised." Ad creative is real Rainkeep gameplay captured from the 3D keep: a sandstorm rolling in at dusk, the player switching the wyrm to Downpour, mist rolling over the keep, villagers safe in the shade. Because the gameplay is real, the ad-to-game gap that drives churn in this genre is closed.
2. **Something new to look at.** A warm, sunlit desert with turquoise water stands out in an ad feed full of snow. It signals "new game" rather than "another clone".
3. **Fresh, fair servers at launch.** Everyone starts on day one, and Oasis Wars spend brackets are announced up front.
4. **The Rainwyrm as the face of the game.** Skins, wyrm-naming, sharing your wyrm's form. A lovable 3D mascot is a UA and social asset that a furnace can't be.
5. **Returning-veteran onboarding.** Skip-tutorial for experienced 4X players and a "Warden's Welcome" catch-up event for anyone who joins a server late.
6. **Creator program** with mid-size strategy and Whiteout Survival YouTubers and streamers at soft launch. Give them early access, custom wyrm skins and Caravan-founder codes.

## 6. From 4.3 to a live multiplayer game

Version 4.16 is a complete single-player game: every system above works offline, with simulated Caravan members and rival keeps. Turning it into a live-service multiplayer game means swapping the simulations for real players:

- **Real Caravans:** shared help requests, donations and Colossus raids backed by a server, plus chat with moderation.
- **Shared Dunes:** one map per server, where caravans can meet rival keeps.
- **Oasis Wars between servers:** real bracketed leaderboards each season, with server transfers at the start of a season.
- **Live ops:** monthly hero banners, new chapters past the Sunheart, seasonal skins and event packs, all tuned through remote config.

## 7. What it takes to ship and scale

This folder is a **complete, playable single-player game** (HTML5 + WebGL, wrapped for iOS/Android with Capacitor), ready for TestFlight playtests and a small-scale store release. A top-grossing live multiplayer game is a much larger project. Rough figures for planning; each should be validated with a studio or publisher:

| Phase | Goal | Typical team / time |
|---|---|---|
| Version 4.16 (this) | Full single-player game in 3D, store-ready shell, simulated multiplayer. Playtest with genre players and soft-launch small | Done; iterate with data.js |
| Live multiplayer | Artist pass over the generated paintings, hero and wyrm animation, server-authoritative backend, real Caravans, shared Dunes, analytics | 6-12 people, 4-6 months |
| Soft launch | 1-3 test markets (commonly Canada, Australia, Philippines). Hit retention and payer gates before scaling spend | 15-30 people, 3-6 months |
| Global launch | Paid user acquisition at scale, live ops, alliances and PvP | 30-80+ people, ongoing |

**Gates before spending real UA money.** These are genre rules of thumb to confirm in soft launch:

- Day-1 retention around 40%, Day-7 around 15%, Day-30 around 6% or better
- A payer conversion rate of a few percent in the first 30 days
- Projected 180-day revenue per install above cost per install with margin. 4X installs in tier-1 markets commonly cost well into double-digit dollars

**What a live backend adds that 4.3 doesn't have:** accounts and cloud saves (4.3 has on-device saves and save codes), server-authoritative timers and economy (4.3 trusts the device, which is normal for a single-player game but not for competitive multiplayer), anti-cheat, analytics events for every economy action, remote config for tuning, push notifications ("Sandstorm in 10 minutes"), chat and moderation.

**Performance.** The 3D scenes are built for phones: static geometry is merged per material so a fully built keep is a few hundred draw calls, there is one shadow-casting light, the shadow map drops to 1024 px on small screens, the pixel ratio is capped at 2, and rendering stops whenever the tab is hidden or another screen is open. Players on older devices can switch 3D off in Settings; the 2D renderer plays the same game.

**Funding and publishing.** Games at Whiteout Survival's revenue are backed by very large user-acquisition budgets. Realistic routes are: (a) take this game to a mobile strategy publisher, or (b) raise for a lean team to reach soft launch and prove the retention gates, then raise or partner for UA.

### Honest risk statement

Most 4X and survival-strategy launches never reach top-grossing charts, regardless of quality, because the category is dominated by a few incumbents with large UA budgets. Nothing in a design document can guarantee revenue. What the plan above does is make the risky bets cheap first (prototype, then soft-launch metrics) and only spend big once players prove they stay and pay.

## 8. Balance and pacing (measured)

The game was tuned with an automated player that plays every system the way a strong, active player would. It builds, researches, trains, recruits, stations stewards, gathers, hunts, explores ruins, donates, raids with its Caravan and claims every reward. It sets the mist before storms (Attuned mist from Lv 6), taps every surplus bubble, calls the rain whenever it's ready, settles incidents, trades with merchants and fights the next stage whenever the odds look good. Since 3.0 it also lights the Forge and forges the cheapest gear piece it can afford, climbs the Mirage Spire when a floor looks winnable, spends every Duel ticket on the best challenger it can beat, and trades Glory for Sunsteel and Epic pouches. Since 3.2 it also spends spare Starglass on supply crates for whatever the next Rainwyrm level is short of and on speedups for long builds, keeping a reserve, and recruits with what is left. A free player can spend Starglass either way, and it makes a big difference, so the table shows both. **Crates or recruits.** A supply crate holds a fixed amount that grows with the Rainwyrm's level, so 100 Starglass pays for most of an early building and still a useful slice of a late one. Recruits make the squad stronger but don't make the keep grow. Players who put Starglass into crates finish the story about 3-4 hours sooner and reach Lv 20 about 10 hours sooner, while Duel and Spire pacing barely changes. That is the intended trade: gacha for collectors and fighters, crates for builders. A player who wants both has a reason to buy more Starglass.

**How 3.0 made the game longer.** The story used to end at stage 60 after about 5 hours. Act II adds four chapters to stage 100, so the story now ends after 11-16 hours (depending on how Starglass is spent), two to three times longer, and the endgame (Lv 20, gear, the Spire) runs to 25-36 hours or more. Act I keeps roughly its old pace (3.5-5 hours to the Sunheart). Past stage 60 the enemy curve is the old Burning Line curve, so players already in the Burning Line drop straight into Act II at a fair difficulty. What carries a player through Act II is Rainwyrm Lv 16-20 (higher hero level caps, troops and research to Lv 15) and the Warden's Gear.

**The gear and the copper economy.** The first draft of gear doubled the squad's power and drew so much copper that building stalled: Act II ended at 10 hours and the wyrm stopped growing. The shipped version gives at most +42% squad attack and defense, +54% health and +30% to each troop class at gear Lv 50, and about a third more with Sunsteel Tempering at Lv 15. Sunsteel is smelted from stone, which piles up late, plus a little copper. Gear upgrades cost Sunsteel and stone. The Forge's racks cap how much Sunsteel waits unspent, so smelting stops instead of draining copper. Copper is still the late-game constraint: stone and food reach the millions while copper stays under 200k. Buildings past Lv 15 take a smaller copper share (13% of their stone cost instead of 22%) to keep the wyrm growing.

**Trials pay out without replacing the story.** The Spire's floor f fights like expedition stage 24 + 0.9f, and its twists make some floors harder than the stage they match. Both trials pay mostly in Sunsteel, Starglass, journals and occasional shard pouches. Their first drafts (an Epic pouch every 10 floors, generous early milestones) pulled Act I down to about 3.3 hours and were cut back.

The Far South's every-tenth-stage bosses are ten named warlords that come back a rank harder; the Crossing has nine mirage events instead of five; two daily duties (cross three rows, refine twice) appear once their modes open. Three Act III heroes join the Beacon once the Ember Throne falls (stage 100), each with a mixed skill like the Act II four: Yusra Tidecaller (legendary bow: attack and healing), Haroun Glasshand (legendary guard: damage reduction and armour-piercing) and Noor Bonewalker (epic lancer: an opening charge), with painted portraits and three-chapter Hero Tales of their own. They widen the pool rather than raise its ceiling, since stats come from rarity. No numbers that pace the game changed.

**Version 4.8 (the keep's look).** The 3D keep was the weakest-looking screen: flat-coloured boxes for buildings, bare streets and villagers made of a few shapes. Buildings now follow their paintings, built from procedural textures (lime plaster, mudbrick, zellige, turquoise glaze, planked wood, lattice and kilim drawn on canvases at load, no new downloads): every house has a stone plinth, a carved door frame, lattice windows that light up at night, and seeded roof life. The Deep Well gains a domed pavilion over the shaft and, at Lv 8, a noria; the archive a tiled iwan; the watchtower a turquoise cupola; the barracks a gate with open doors; and so on through all twelve. The streets gain a zellige ring around the spring, three market stalls with their sellers, string lanterns, flowers and rugs on the terrace walls, carts, teal-domed gate towers and a tiled Rain Altar. Villagers are single skinned meshes (body, arms and legs on bones) with varied clothing, headwear and things to carry. To keep the frame cheap, `bake()` now merges every opaque material from `mat()` that differs only in colour into one vertex-coloured mesh, so the keep draws in about 520 calls at Lv 12, down from about 620 in 4.7, despite the extra detail. No gameplay numbers changed.

**Version 4.9 (Companions, the painted 3D cast).** Companions give the middle game a collection to grow alongside the heroes, with their own two currencies. The balance bot tames each one as soon as it can (it saves treats for the next one rather than feeding), feeds the rest, advances, and fires every skill when it is ready. Its first runs ended with thousands of unspent treats and no bells, then with bells piling up while it never saved enough to tame, so beasts now drop treats less often, levels cost more as they climb, and bells come from more places. In the last free-to-play run Sahra arrived at minute 23, the second companion at 123, the third at 192, the fourth at 250, the fifth at 503 and the last at 910, ending with a handful of treats and 36 bells in hand. Free-to-play pacing is unchanged: Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,316 to 1,401 minutes (about 1,350 in 4.7), and the whale reaches Lv 20 at 799 minutes and stage 150 at 1,010 (801 and 1,026 before). The keep's people and animals are now the painted 3D models. Heroes, who had no presence in the keep, now stand in it: each Steward by the building they look after and the squad in the plaza, pacing a few steps now and then; a tap opens their sheet, and a figure inside a building's tap box gets the tap over the building. The camels on the caravan road, the raiders who march on the gate and the animals of the Dunes are painted too (the Dust Wraith stays a drawn spirit; the tortoise and the vultures had been drawn as a stone golem and ghosts). At Lv 12 with nine Stewards, three squad heroes, six companions and 22 villagers the keep draws in about 495 calls (528 with the drawn figures, as a painted camel is one mesh where a drawn one was a dozen) and about 410,000 triangles (260,000). The models ship as separate files, not inlined, so the page itself stays under 8 MB and returning players get them from the offline cache.

**Version 4.40 (Breath Arts).** The Rainwyrm's breath was the same in every fight: a Torrent, best spent on a wind-up. From Rainwyrm Lv 6 it can be a Mist Veil (it heals the squad, softens the next two blows and draws out venom) and from Lv 11 a Riptide (40% of a Torrent's damage, but the foe is held under for two rounds: no blows, no regeneration). Every art breaks a wind-up, so the first lesson of the live battle still holds, and each answers different traits, so the foe decides the breath as well as the squad. The arts apply where the choice is offered (expedition stages, star replays and the Leviathan); elsewhere the breath stays a Torrent, because in the Crossing and the Siege, where health carries from fight to fight, a Mist Veil would have been a free oasis every fight. The numbers came from a simulation like the one for foe traits: for a plain foe and each trait, early and late, the squad strength each art needs to win half its fights, with a squad of Guard heroes so the breath is the only answer. The first Veil healed twice the breath's share and needed 6% less strength than a Torrent even against plain foes; the first Riptide (half damage, one round) was barely better than a Torrent anywhere, regeneration included. At the shipped numbers all three need within a few percent of each other on a plain foe, the Veil needs about 7% less against venom, the Riptide about 4% less against regeneration, and the Torrent stays ahead against shells and frenzy. Where the arts matter more is the Unbroken star: a squad 15% stronger than it needs almost never ends a fight above half health with a Torrent, but often does with a Veil (or with a Riptide against regeneration). Played by the bots, which pick the art that does best over a few simulated fights, the Torrent is still chosen 92% of the time, the Veil 6% and the Riptide 2%; against a run without arts the free bot reached stage 150 about 5% sooner, with the same Hall rank (14th) and the same 189 wins.

**Version 4.39 (Stage Stars).** The 150 story stages were fought once and left behind, and the live battle's tactics (the breath into a wind-up, the skills' timing) only ever decided whether a marginal fight was won. Every story stage now holds three stars: the victory, ending with half the squad's health (Unbroken) and winning within five rounds (Swift). A first clear at the edge of the squad's strength rarely takes all three, so the stages behind the player become something to come back to as the squad grows, and the live battle is how the last star is taken early. Replays pay only their stars, so there is nothing to farm, and the chapter's thirty stars fill three chests. The thresholds came from a simulation of auto-battles at five stages through the game, measured against the squad strength that wins half its fights. At first Swift was six rounds, and it said nothing: fights at the edge of the squad's strength already end in five to seven rounds, so every win was swift. At five rounds a first clear at that edge takes about one star (two in the first chapters), at 15% more strength about two, and all three at about 30% more (50% in Act III, where fights run longer); Unbroken is usually the harder star: a squad 15% stronger than it needs still ends most fights below half health, though against a few Act III bosses that hit hard and late it is the easier one. The bots finished all 450 stars and 45 chests within 30 hours of play, with 57 (whale) to 125 (free) replays, so the stars follow the story rather than outlasting it. The chests are a modest extra: about 1,350 Starglass, 15 Beacon Tokens and 45 Desert Whetstones over the whole story, against roughly 7,500 Starglass from first clears.

**Version 4.38 (The Sparring Ring, and a review).** Heroes off the squad fell far behind it, because the squad gets the journals, so a kinship partner, a hero whose skill answers a foe's trait or a party for a far journey was rarely worth fielding: the bots in 4.36 never once took the trait swap, because the hero who could answer was always too weak to be worth it. From Rainwyrm Lv 10 up to four heroes (six with Starglass) sit in the Sparring Ring and fight at the level of the keep's third-best hero outside the ring, never past their own level cap, so stars still matter. The first version measured the ring against the squad's weakest hero and gave a seat up when its hero joined the squad, which took the bonus away at the moment it was meant for; a review caught it, and the ring now measures against the best three heroes outside it, so a seated hero keeps the level in the squad, on a march or anywhere else. The same review fixed four smaller things. The Sand Leviathan took on the boss strength and traits of whatever stage the keep was on, so it is now built from its hunt's own stage as plain stats. Its par was measured with whoever was home when it surfaced, so par is now set by the attacks themselves; a second review found that a weak squad sent first could then set it low for the real attacks to beat (and take first place and every mark), so par only ever rises to what the strongest attacking squad does on auto-battle. An attack still going when the hunt rolls over counts for nothing. The wardens it ranks you among were measured against the keep's power rather than the Hall's own figure. The trait swap could pull out a hero who was away on a march, or the only answer to the foe's other trait, and kinship fights counted marches and raids as squad fights. Before the ring the bots never once took the trait swap in three runs; with it the free bot took it 419 and 291 times in two runs of 30 hours, and not at all in a third whose roster happened to have no answerer close enough in strength, so the ring does what it was for when the heroes are there. The Leviathan's fixes moved the free bot's final rank from 28th to 14th, close to the 15th the design aims at for a good free player, and the Hall rank barely moved (15th against 16th).

**Version 4.37 (Ready now, and the Hall redrawn).** With forty-odd features each marking itself with a dot, the rail and its hubs said that something was waiting but not what, and a player had to open hub after hub to find it. Ready now gathers every lit dot into one list, along with the things the keep itself waits on (idle builders, an idle Archive or Barracks, a quest to claim), with a button to each and a way back to the list; in a fresh keep at Rainwyrm Lv 12 it lists about eighteen. The Hall of Wardens was redrawn in the same release. It counts troops out marching and holding outposts, which its first curve (drawn in 4.19, before outposts) never saw, and the free bots had drifted to rank 5 or 6 where the design puts a good free player near 15, so the daily payout and the Leviathan's ranks were both generous. The bots now log the Hall's own figure hour by hour, and the new curve is theirs: about 1.7 times the old one from the third hour on, which puts a player at the curve 17th or 18th. With the new curve the free bots finished their runs at ranks 15 and 16 and a spending run at 13.

**Version 4.36 (Foe traits).** Expedition fights were a stat check with a class counter and two timing decisions (the breath, the skills), the same for every foe. From stage 16 each foe now has a trait, and bosses two, and each trait has an answer the squad can bring: Sunder cuts twice as deep through armor, a skill that strikes stops regeneration for the round, Mend draws venom out and wards against it, and the Torrent cracks a sand-shell or calms a frenzy. So the squad wants a spread of skills rather than the three strongest heroes, the breath has a reason to wait, and the stage card says before the fight whether the squad can answer (with a one-tap swap for the strongest free hero who can). The numbers came from a simulation rather than the bots, because the bots move too much from run to run to see a few percent: for five stages through the game, the strength a squad needs to win half its fights against each trait, against a plain foe, with and without the answer. At first every trait made the foe 6% weaker to pay for itself, which made the Torrent's two traits free wins (every squad has the breath, so they cost nothing); frenzy also built too slowly and regeneration was too strong even when answered. With the ease only on the skill-answered traits (now 4%), a frenzy of 10% a round and regeneration of 5%, an answered trait needs about the same strength as a plain foe and an unanswered one 3% to 10% more, and the breath traits break even if the breath is spent on them. The bots then showed the other side of the decision: they were taught to swap in a hero who answers a trait, but in over 3,000 checks in a short run they never found one worth it, because a benched hero is levelled far behind the squad and costs the squad more than 5% of its power, troops and all. So the swap on the stage card says what it does to the squad's power, and the real answer is a squad levelled with a spread of skills. Pacing held: free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,287 and 1,402 minutes (stage 150 at 779 and 1,013) and a spending run at 911. From its second hunt the Leviathan carries a trait too, with regeneration sized from a normal foe's health like the breath.

**Version 4.35 (Relic Charms).** The Buried City paid as it was dug, but a relic once dug up was only a line in the Relic Hall, and the twentieth Clay Lamp meant nothing. Charms give every relic a use: each kind can be worn by one hero, and its level is the number of that kind found, so duplicates are progress and the dig feeds the squad. The bigger the relic, the more a level gives (a Clay Lamp 1% health a level, the grand relics 2% to 2.5%), so the rare finds are worth chasing, and one charm per hero with one hero per charm turns the thirteen kinds into a small allocation puzzle (the Star Tablet's skill power on the hero whose skill matters most, the Sun Chariot's attack on the counter hero). The bots dug up enough to raise their best charms to Lv 7 to 9 in 30 hours and put them on the squad. At first the charms gave half as much again (a Clay Lamp 1.5% a level, the Star Tablet 4%), and the free runs finished the story two hours sooner for it (stage 150 at 876 and 897 minutes against 1,011 with charms switched off in the same build); battle features have been shortening the story version by version, so the charms are now 40% weaker. The Rainwyrm, which the economy paces, did not move (Lv 20 at 1,385 to 1,580 minutes free, 884 for a spending run).

**Version 4.34 (the Sand Leviathan).** Every competitive mode so far rewarded strength first, and the Hall of Wardens ranks power alone. The Leviathan, after the bear hunts and mercenary bosses of games like this one, is a fixed target scored by damage, so an attack rewards playing the fight well: it winds up every third round like any boss, it fights as one troop class each hunt so the counter squad does most, and a squad that guards and breathes into the wind-ups lasts all twelve rounds where one on auto-battle falls back early. Par (the squad's auto-battle damage when the hunt begins) gives a personal target whose marks pay as soon as they are beaten, and the Hall's fifty wardens give the ranking: their attacks are their share of your par by power, with luck on the day, so the board moves with you and rank tracks strength while the marks track skill. Its health is twelve stage foes, enough that no squad at its own frontier can finish it but the bar still moves. The breath had to be sized from a normal foe's health: measured against the Leviathan's it did more than the rest of the fight put together. In the 30-hour runs the Leviathan opens within the first hour (Rainwyrm Lv 9 at 45 to 49 minutes free) and the bots attack it six times over two hunts; on auto-battle they land close to par, and a free run finished its first hunt at the top of the board. Rank follows strength relative to the Hall's curve, which was drawn from older, weaker bot runs, so the Hall itself may want redrawing as the game grows.

**Version 4.33 (Hero Kinships).** Squads were the three strongest heroes and nothing else. Twelve kinships, pairs whose stories already touched (the Glasshands, the Wyrm-Singers, the Keep Kitchen), make the question sometimes a pair and the best third, and give a reason to recruit and star a particular hero. Each hero is in one pair at most, so a squad of three has at most one kinship at work and the bonus stays bounded (5% to 9% at first, up to double at ten stars between them); the humbler pairs give more so that rare heroes have a squad role late in the game. The bots form 11 or 12 kinships in 30 hours and field one whenever the squad comes out stronger for it, which is most of the time once two of a pair are starred (the Gatekeepers, Shield and Shade and the Dune Hunters most of all). With kinships on, the Rainwyrm's pace held: Lv 20 at 1,448 and 1,480 minutes free (1,441 with the Founding Week, kinships and the Leviathan switched off) and 892 for a spending run.

**Version 4.32 (the Founding Week).** The game had chapter quests, daily duties and a login calendar, but nothing that shaped a new player's first week or promised something big at the end of it, which is where games of this kind keep or lose most of their players. The Founding Week gives each of the first seven days five missions that walk through the core loop as it opens (building, the expedition, recruiting, troops, the Dunes, the Cookfire, Far Journeys, the first raid on a rival), with chests along a 700-point track and a Legendary hero of the player's choosing at the end. A day opens each real day, or after each 24 hours of keep time for a player who plays long sessions, and the week stays open two days past the seventh so one missed day can be caught up. Only a keep no further than Rainwyrm Lv 6 when it would open gets the week, so an existing save is never handed a week of missions already done. A Founding Chest ($4.99, once, during the week) is the first-week offer. A 30-hour bot sees two days of keep time, collects all ten of their missions and opens the first two chests; the rest of the week is for real days.

**Version 4.31 (the Buried City).** The keep has plenty of things that are fast and plenty that are long, but few that ask the player to think for a minute, and the bots show that the late game piles up rewards with nothing to spend attention on. The Buried City is a small deduction puzzle with a slow, free supply of moves: dug sand counts the relic pieces in the eight tiles around it, the relics' sizes are known, and the player works out where they must lie. A simulation of the rules set the grid: on a 6x7 layer with five relics (17 tiles of 42), a digger who reads the sand clears a layer in about 24 Trowels and one who digs at random in about 41, so reading the sand saves two layers' worth of Trowels in five, a gap wide enough to feel. Bedrock from layer 4 and pay that grows 4% a layer keep the deeper layers interesting, a Blasting Charge is the satisfying shortcut, and the Relic Hall gives the thirteen named relics a collection to fill. Trowels come free every 45 minutes (up to 12), from bosses, from the chests and from the $1.99 Digger's Kit. The bots read the sand the way a careful player would and used about 25 Trowels a layer, as the simulation said, but half their Trowels came from beast hunts (the bot kills about 70 beasts an hour), which took them through 9 to 13 layers in 30 hours and past the Legendary pouch at layer 10 within a day. Beasts now drop a Trowel one time in 33 rather than one in 17, so the pouch is a chase of several days for a player. Pacing held with the 4.29 and 4.30 trims: the free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,484 and 1,531 minutes (stage 150 at 1,009 and 1,042), and spending runs at 899 minutes with the dig and 820 with it switched off, inside the spending runs' usual spread. Outside the gate a dig site appears in the keep, a timber hoist over a shaft with spoil heaps and an awning over the finds, and tapping it opens the dig.

**Version 4.30 (the Cookfire).** Fishing was a pastime whose catches only filled a book. The Cookfire makes every catch an ingredient, so the spring feeds the rest of the keep: four minnows are an afternoon's faster gathering, a Glass Eel is half the sick back on their feet after a sandstorm, and the rare Rain Koi is a banquet. Two dishes on the table, never the same one twice, makes a small choice every time the table empties (what does the keep need for the next few hours?), and the larder fills from play the player already does, so there is no new currency to farm. The dishes are timed rather than permanent so the bonus follows attention, like the decrees. The bots cooked about 20 dishes in 30 hours and kept a Rain Koi Banquet on the table for most of the run (seven banquets), so the banquet's production share came down from 10% to 6%; its battle half (squads 8% harder, the Torrent +10%) is what makes it the best dish now.

**Version 4.29 (Building Charters).** Past Lv 10 a building was only a bigger number. A charter gives each one an identity chosen once: more of what it makes, or something else the keep needs (a Deep Well's Artesian Bore or its Covered Cisterns, a Barracks' Drill Yard or its Quartermaster). The first choice is free and changing costs 100 Starglass, so trying one is never punished but swapping back and forth is not free; the two Mudbrick Houses share one so a keep's character stays readable. Each pair is meant to be a real decision, which the first numbers did not manage: the bots take the production charter wherever there is one, and with Journeys, Charters and the Cookfire together the free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 about 40 minutes sooner (1,390 and 1,453 minutes against 1,489 with all three switched off) and a spending run 100 minutes sooner (792 against 857 to 955 in earlier versions). Production charters are now +8% (from 12%), and the Storehouse's Tally Clerks +2% (from 3%), which leaves Covered Cisterns, the Masons' Guild and the rest as close calls rather than traps.

**Version 4.28 (Far Journeys).** Heroes off the squad had errands and nothing else, and recruiting a fourth Shieldbearer did nothing for a keep that already had one. Journeys, after the hero dispatches of games like this one, give the whole roster work: a board of five, each with one to three requirements the party must meet together (two of a class, so many stars, a Legendary, a hero of some level, a full party of three), more for the longer, richer journeys. The board is drawn so the keep's own heroes can meet every journey on it, so a requirement is a puzzle of who to send rather than a wall, and the party picker suggests the best party that spares the squad. Heroes away can't fight, which is the cost, and every hero in a party comes home with shards of their own, which is why a deep roster pays. The bots sent 10 to 14 journeys in 30 hours (mostly one- and two-star; four-star journeys come one in ten), all came home, and the story and the Rainwyrm kept their pace (see 4.29 for the three versions' numbers together).

**Version 4.27 (the Dry Season).** The keep is about water, but after the first hours water stopped being a worry: every bot run ends with hundreds of thousands in store and has not gone thirsty in many versions. The Dry Season brings the founding pressure back on a schedule, in the spirit of the blizzards that games of this kind are built around: every three days the wells sink, people drink more and the air is hotter, for half a day, with two hours' warning. Each edict answers it at a different cost (rationing costs production, a cistern costs stone and copper but leaves water behind for good, the rain watch costs the Torrent in battle), so the right one depends on what the keep is short of. The grade and its chest reward coming through well rather than merely surviving. The first numbers (wells -20%, drinking +10%, +2°C) never made a bot run thirsty, edict or not, so every season graded A; they are now -25%, +15% and +3°C, which a cistern answers in full and a keep that ignores the season can feel. In 30-hour runs the bots meet one season (they dig a cistern and earn an A), and pacing held: free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,426 to 1,541 minutes against 1,499 with the season switched off, and spending runs at 902.

**Version 4.26 (Pacts & Feuds).** Rival keeps were targets and nothing else: the bots raided the weakest one in reach again and again, and a raided keep forgot it after one strike back. Regard gives every raid a cost that lasts and every keep a second use. A gift moves a keep a little and only every few hours, so a pact is something built over a day, and a raid undoes almost two gifts. Pacts are strong but few (one at Rainwyrm Lv 8, three by Lv 16) and they are with the keeps you would otherwise fear, so the natural play is to befriend the strong and farm the weak, which is the trade the genre's alliance diplomacy is about, with a price for farming: a keep driven into a feud sends warbands on its own. The first numbers (a pact at 60, gifts of 15 every 8 hours) were too slow: the bots gifted the strongest keep eleven times in 30 hours and never reached a pact. At 50, with gifts of 20 every 6 hours, the bots sign their first pact about 12 hours into the game and hold all three by the end; the two keeps they farm sink into feuds and send 8 or 9 warbands between them (against about 6 strike-backs before), which allies on the walls help beat off (89 of 90 raids held in a spending run). Pacing held: free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,463 and 1,459 minutes and a spending run at 887, with stage 150 at 1,074, 1,121 and 565.

**Version 4.25 (the Camel Derby).** Fishing and Cloud Run are skill games with no rivals; the derby is a race against five, with a camel to raise between races, so it has both a long progression (three stats to Lv 20, about 57 hours of training in all) and a short, readable skill. The one control is holding to urge, and the skill is in the budget: urging is fast and holds the pace over the dunes, but burns energy, and a camel that runs dry is slow for a few seconds, so the race rewards riding the dunes and the home straight and resting on the hard pan. The rivals ride three ways (sprinters who go early, closers who wait, steady riders), so the pack moves differently every race. A simulation of the race rules set the numbers before any art was drawn: at the first pass a single level of Speed decided too much (a camel in the middle of a cup's range almost never won, one at the top almost always did), so each level now counts for less and every camel gets a small random form on the day. A camel at the top of a cup's range now wins about four races in five ridden well and two in five ridden badly, and one in the middle about one in six, so riding matters as much as a few levels. Cups open one after another on a first win, each paying a piece of tack, so a player always has a next goal. In the bot runs Saffron trained to Lv 14 or 15 in every stat, won the Village Cup and then the Oasis Stakes three or four times in 10 races. Training first cost food and water, and the bots showed water running short in the middle of the game, with the Rainwyrm reaching Lv 20 about 40 minutes late, so training now costs food alone (the bots end every run with millions of it).

**Version 4.24 (Hero Talents).** Heroes grew by levels, stars, heirlooms and awakening, all of which only make a hero more of the same. Talents add the first choices: at each tier a hero takes something for themselves (attack, defense or health) or something for the whole fight (leading their class's troops, a stronger or earlier skill, a bigger edge over the class they beat, a stronger Torrent). Both halves of each pair are meant to be right sometimes: a hero leading a march of their own class wants Shield Captain, one in a mixed squad may not. The talents are per class rather than per hero so that thirty names and ten effects stay readable, and the first choice is free so a player is never punished for trying one; only changing it costs Starglass. Since talents only make fights easier, the bots' story moved sooner and the Rainwyrm, which the economy paces, did not: stage 150 at 635 to 691 minutes for spending runs against 738 with talents and the Derby switched off, and at 1,110 to 1,171 for free runs against 1,155.

**Version 4.23 (Warden's Decrees).** Every timer in the keep ran on its own, and the only lever a player had over them was Starglass. Decrees, after the Chief Orders of games like this one, give a free lever with a decision in it: each one is strong for a short while and then rests for many hours, so the question is never whether to give it but when (a Harvest Rite before a long time away, a Call to Arms before a raid or a clash, a Feast of Rain after a sandstorm leaves people sick). The rests run from when a decree ends, not when it starts, so the effects can't overlap themselves, and they are long enough (8 to 24 hours) that a decree is an event in a day rather than a button to press every visit. Re-issuing early costs Starglass by the hours of rest left, a sink that buys a little and never stacks. The effects are kept small on purpose because the bots give every decree the moment it is ready, as an attentive player would. The bots showed that a Rush Order rarely had much to cut: with the Caravan's help and the speedups the bots' timers seldom had more than 25 minutes left, so the bot now gives it once 20 minutes are under way, about twice a run; a player who builds overnight will get more from it. In the 30-hour runs the bots gave 14 to 16 decrees and pacing did not move: the free runs reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,454 and 1,452 minutes (1,456 and 1,458 in 4.22), and three spending runs at 914, 954 and 955 against 881, 901 and 943 with decrees switched off, inside the run-to-run spread, with stage 150 at 668 to 684 either way. In the keep, a Harvest Rite or a Feast strings bunting round the spring's plaza and a Call to Arms raises red banners on the wall towers.

**Version 4.22 (Clash League).** The Wadi Clash paid by place but nothing carried from one match to the next, and a mode played every four hours needs a ladder to climb. League points are weighted so that a second place still climbs and a loss costs half a win (+40, +10, -20), and the floor of each tier means a bad run never takes a promotion back, which matters in a mode with this much luck in it. Each tier makes the rivals 5% stronger, so the ladder settles where the player's skill and strength put them instead of rising forever, and 15% richer match rewards make every step worth having. A simulation of the clash rules with the captains' auto-player settled the numbers: in a week's 42 matches (one banner every 4 hours) the captains end the season around Gold, a squad 10% stronger reaches Sunsteel and one 20% stronger reaches Rainwyrm; at 8% or 10% a tier even the strongest stalled at Sunsteel. A season pays by the highest tier reached rather than the last, so a slump at the end never costs the reward, and the next season starts two tiers lower so there is always a climb. In the 30-hour bot runs ten matches took the captains to Copper and Silver (160 to 270 points), and the story and the Rainwyrm kept their pace (free runs: stage 150 at 1,080 and 1,255 minutes, Lv 20 at 1,456 and 1,458).

**Version 4.21 (Trade Routes).** Every balance run ends with the keep holding millions of food and stone it has no use for, while the things that gate the late game (whetstones, Sunsteel, speedups, shards) only drip in. Trade turns the one into the other at a rate that stays a decision: the goods asked for scale with the keep, the payment is fixed in items, and each market's prices move from day to day, so the same order is a bargain one day and poor the next. Distance trades time and risk for value, and guards are a fee: pay a little more for a safe road or gamble on it. Starglass appears only on the longest road and at a rate far below what the Store charges for crates, so trade can't be used to mint premium currency. The first escorts were troops, sent with the caravan and home with it. The bots showed why that fails: with outposts already holding garrisons, three escorted caravans on the road left expedition battles short-handed, and spending runs reached stage 150 two hours late, while the same runs trading without escorts reached it two hours early. Guards are now hired for copper and food (half or full, full making the road safe), so trade costs goods and never the army. The bots send 16 to 18 caravans home in 30 hours, the story and the Rainwyrm keep their pace (free runs: stage 150 at 1,186 minutes, Lv 20 at 1,451; spending runs: stage 150 at 755 and 844, Lv 20 at 870 and 891, against 754 and 855 with trade switched off), and a third of the Glass Cities' orders still go out unguarded when copper runs short.

**Version 4.20 (Oasis Outposts).** On the Dunes everything was a visit: march out, gather, fight, come home, and the tile forgets you. Holding ground is the heart of map strategy, so outposts let a node become yours for as long as you can keep it, and turn troops into a standing commitment: every soldier in a garrison is one not gathering, not defending the keep and not marching on a rival. Raiders are sized from your own march cap and Barracks level rather than from the map, so the question is always the same and easy to read (did I leave enough, and are my walls high enough?), while Troop Ranks and Warden's Gear quietly tilt it in your favour. The ten-minute warning and the reinforcement button give a reason to look at the map between sessions without punishing an absent player too hard: an outpost that falls costs half its garrison and a day's yield, never the keep. The first raider bands (a fifth to two fifths of the march cap) were too big: the bots held four outposts with garrisons that together outnumbered a full march, so expedition battles went out short-handed and the story ran four hours late. Bands are now a tenth to a fifth of the march cap, so four garrisons that hold still leave a full march at home (troop housing is twice the march cap). The bots then raise all four outposts, take them to Lv 5, beat off 13 to 17 raids in 30 hours and lose none; the free runs reach stage 150 at 1,211 and 1,224 minutes and Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,485 and 1,562, inside their usual spread. A spending run with outposts switched off reached Lv 20 at 867 minutes against 874 and 906 with them, so outposts cost the Rainwyrm nothing and the story at most half an hour.

**Version 4.19 (Hero Awakening).** The spending bot ended every 30-hour run with all 25 heroes at 5 stars, and from then on every recruit it bought turned into shards with no use. In games of this kind the hero ladder past the top star is where the biggest spenders go, so awakening gives those shards a home without touching anyone below 5 stars. Costs are counted in duplicates rather than raw shards so a Rare and a Legendary take the same number of recruits (20 duplicates for A5). The gains are deliberately smaller per step than a star (6% stats against a star's 15%) because they stack on top of everything else; the skill steps at A3 and A5 are the ones players will chase. The first numbers were far too generous: a free player buys about 1,300 recruits in 30 hours, roughly fifty duplicates per hero, so the free bots took fifteen heroes to A5 and finished the story a quarter sooner. Each step now opens at a Rainwyrm level (A1 at 12 up to A5 at 20) and costs 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 duplicates, and the steps give 4% stats and 10% and 20% skill. The bots then awaken their squad to A5 by Rainwyrm Lv 20 and seven to nine heroes in all, and the story and the Rainwyrm keep their usual pace (stage 150 at 1,139 and 1,314 minutes for the free runs, Lv 20 at 1,398 and 1,587).

**Hall of Wardens (with 4.19).** Power was a number on the hero screen and nothing more. Games of this kind put it on a public ladder, because climbing past named rivals is a goal that never ends and pays a little every day. Without real players, the 49 rivals follow the balance bot's own power curve (sampled every half hour over two free runs, smoothed so it never falls), each at a fixed share and pace, so the ladder means the same thing at hour 2 as at hour 200. The shares are packed low (a free player who plays well sits near rank 15) and the top is a little above the spending bot (which sits near rank 6), so first place is possible but asks for more than steady play. Your power counts troops out marching, and only a new best rank is announced, so the ladder never nags when troops come and go.

**Version 4.18 (Wadi Clash).** Every fight in the game so far was resolved by numbers: you chose who went, and the engine decided. The Wadi Clash is the first battle where position and timing decide it, in the spirit of the canyon battles that keep strategy players in these games coming back on schedule. It is kept to three minutes and three squads so it fits a phone session, and it plays on a 2D map of seven points rather than in 3D so every decision is readable at a glance. Fights use Lanchester's square law, which rewards the one real tactic (concentrate, and never feed squads in one at a time) and is easy to feel without being told. The rivals' captains go after whoever is leading, so a runaway lead invites a counterattack. The map is not symmetric: your camp sits alone at the bottom with its own tower, while the rival camps share the top and fight each other over the Rain Shrine. Even with the rivals as strong as you, a simulation of 400 matches had your squads' captains (the same AI the rivals use) winning four in five, so the rivals are now 0 to 30% stronger than your squads and the goal is 700. The auto-player then places first about 40% of the time, second 43% and third 17%, and a player who concentrates squads should do better. In the 30-hour bot runs the clash cost the Rainwyrm nothing (free runs reached Lv 20 at 1,453 and 1,490 minutes, a spending run at 831) and the captains won 8 of 15 matches. They also showed the first cadence, a banner every 8 hours, gave only five matches in 30 hours, too few for a mode that needs practice, so banners now come every 4 hours, three at most.

**Version 4.17 (Troop Ranks).** Troops were the one part of an army that never grew on their own: the Barracks level and research made every troop stronger at once, and past Barracks Lv 20 nothing did. In games of this kind troop tiers are the longest progression and the deepest resource sink, and the bots ended every 30-hour run holding millions of food and stone with nothing to spend them on. Ranks add both: a drill costs the class's own training resources (so archers ask for stone, lancers for copper) times a share of a crate that grows with the keep, and takes the Barracks, so training and drilling compete for the same building. Two choices keep it simple to read and fair. A class fights at the average of its troops rather than sending its best first, so there is no per-march bookkeeping and every drill visibly lifts the number on the class row. And recruits fall first, so a lost fight costs the cheap troops and the ranks you paid for survive as long as you keep the Barracks full. The first numbers (+20%, +45%, +75%) were too strong: in the bots Champions arrived at Barracks Lv 18, just before the Act III finale, and free runs reached stage 150 five hours sooner. Dearer drills fixed the stages but took the resources the Rainwyrm needed and slowed it by up to two hours. The ranks now give +10%, +20% and +32% at the first costs. In three 30-hour runs every troop ends a Champion, the Rainwyrm keeps its pace (free runs reach Lv 20 at 1,436 and 1,464 minutes, a spending run at 863), and the story moves about an eighth sooner (stage 150 at about 1,210 minutes for free players instead of about 1,390, and 775 for a spender instead of 908 to 959). Stronger troops are the point of the system, so the story is allowed to move a little faster. The Rainwyrm, which the economy is paced around, stays the long pole.

**Version 4.16 (Gate Defenses).** The gate is where three systems meet (raids from 4.0, rival warbands from 4.11 and the siege from 4.12), but it had nothing of its own to build. Three works give the stone and copper surplus of the middle game a home that pays back in those fights, and the siege's extra tactics at Lv 5 and 10 turn "three of each for ten waves" into a long-term goal rather than a fixed limit. In the balance bot every work reached Lv 10 in all four 30-hour runs (two free, two spending), the Scorpion king fell in 3 to 5 sieges of 5, and the time to Rainwyrm Lv 20 stayed inside the usual spread (free runs 1,390 and 1,432 minutes, spending runs 784 and 862) even with the stone and copper the works take. The 3D works were first too fine to see from the keep camera (a 7 cm stake is invisible from fifty metres), so the stakes, ballistas and cauldrons are now drawn about twice the size of life.

**Version 4.15 (Spring Fishing).** A mini-game for the first hours (Rainwyrm Lv 3), when there is little to do while timers run, that also ties back to the keep's theme: the rain brought the water back, and fish with it, and calling the rain brings more casts and the rare Rain Koi. The skill is in letting go: holding through a run triples the tension, so the player reads the fish rather than mashing. The bots cast about 1,360 times in 30 hours and landed about three in four. They also showed a flaw: no run ever landed an Old Whiskers or a Rain Koi. A simulation of the reel confirmed that nothing pulling harder than about 1.6 can be landed even with perfect timing, because the fish runs more often and longer the harder it pulls and every run takes back more than holding gains. The two pulled 2.0 and 2.4. They now pull 1.52 and 1.55, just under the edge: a steady hand (a fifth of a second to react) lands them about nine times in ten and three in four, a hasty one rarely, and the auto-player about half and a fifth of the time.

**Version 4.14 (Heirlooms, Formations, What's new).** The modes added in 4.10 to 4.13 each paid their own way (Road Dice, Starglass, survivors, shards) but shared no progression, and heroes off the squad had little reason to grow. Heirlooms tie them together: the siege and Crossing chests, watchtower errands and bounties, and the Bazaar all pay Desert Whetstones, and tempering is per hero, so a player chooses who to invest in. The numbers are deliberately small per level (2.5% stats, 4% skill) so ten levels on the squad matter without outranking stars. The balance bot tempers only its squad's heirlooms (only the squad fights; a first pass that tempered every hero at 3 stars spread the whetstones over a dozen benched heroes at Lv 1 or 2). It gathered about 150 to 190 whetstones in 30 hours and took the three squad heirlooms to Lv 4 or 5, a quarter of the way to a fully tempered squad (762), and it takes the formation the picker rates best for every fight. Pacing held: Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,306 to 1,375 minutes free-to-play and 719 to 780 for the whale. Formations give every fight a small decision: the counter class hits 20% harder, but a Shieldbearer lead trades attack for staying power, so the picker forecasts each formation with the battle engine and marks the best rather than telling the player to always take the counter. What's new exists because new features unlock deep into the game and are easy to miss: a returning player now sees them once, with a button to each.

**Version 4.13 (Watchtower Intel).** The Dunes filled up with things to do (nodes, beasts, camps, ruins, rival keeps), but none of them said "today, this". Intel reports do: a short ledger of star-rated jobs with a story each, refreshing through the day, that point the player at the map and give the benched heroes something to do (an errand pays the hero's own shards, so a second-string hero still grows). Reports sit over the cached tile layout rather than in it, so one can come and go on any patch of open sand without disturbing the seeded map. The balance bot answers every caravan, relic and errand when a march slot is free (holding one slot back while a report waits) and every fight its full march outmatches by 15%. Its first pass found a real problem: the harder reports (fights just past the expedition) sat on the ledger and blocked new ones, and only 3 to 10 were answered in 30 hours. So reports now fade after 6 hours and fights sit a little below the expedition, and the bot answered 13 to 15 free-to-play (14 for the whale, three of them five-star). Pacing did not move: Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,291 and 1,484 minutes with Intel and 1,293 and 1,448 without it in the same build (the bot's run-to-run spread is wider than one pair of runs suggests), and 767 for the whale.

**Version 4.12 (the Scorpion Siege).** Raids on the gate were a single fight decided by strength. The siege turns the gate into a mode with decisions: which counter to spend on which wave when there are only three of each for ten waves, whether to boil water for the walls, when Starglass is worth an extra Ballista for the King. Because the host measures itself against the defenders on the walls, strength alone doesn't win it; hero skills (heals, damage reduction, bursts), the breath, the Watchtower and the Healer's House do, along with the tactics. A calibration of 60 auto-played sieges per case at three keep sizes picked the curve: with no tactics the King almost never falls, and countering every wave with cauldrons beats him more often as the keep grows. In the 30-hour free-to-play bot (which counters every wave and boils water) the first two sieges, at Rainwyrm Lv 10 and 11, ended at waves 8 and 9; the next three beat the King. Pacing did not move. A first pair of runs looked about 50 minutes slower, so the bot played four more (two with the siege, two without): across five free-to-play runs with the siege, Rainwyrm Lv 20 came at 1,346 to 1,403 minutes, and at 1,309 to 1,393 in four without it (4.11 included), so the difference is run-to-run noise. The whale reached Lv 20 at 768 and 817 minutes (774 in 4.11) and beat the King in every siege.

**Version 4.11 (Rival Keeps).** The Dunes had beasts, camps and ruins but nobody else's keep, so the genre's central loop (scout a city, raid it for resources, fear the raid back, raise a shield) was missing. Eight simulated rival keeps now give it a shape: a warden, a colour, a strength tied to your own progress (0.7 to 1.5 of your expedition stage, so there is always someone weaker to raid and someone stronger to watch), a storehouse that refills, and a temper. A rival you raid may send its warband back as the next raid on your gate, which reuses Hold the Gate (warning, rain, boiling water) instead of adding a second defence system, and revenge within the hour pays +20% attack. The Peace Shield is a Starglass sink with a real trade-off: it drops the moment you attack. The balance bot raids the richest rival its march outmatches by a quarter, when the squad is home and no raiders are in sight. It won 17 raids in a 30-hour free-to-play run (13 for the whale), lost none and was struck back 4 times, and pacing did not move: Rainwyrm Lv 20 at 1,309 minutes free-to-play (1,329 to 1,348 before) and 774 for the whale (746).

**Version 4.10 (the Spice Road).** A dice board, the genre's best-loved event shape (Whiteout Survival's Tundra Adventure), as a standing feature with 8-hour seasons. It gives every session a quick, lucky moment that needs no planning, a reason to visit the duty chests and the expedition bosses, and a daily $1.99 pack with a Lucky Die, the lever that makes a dice board pay. The balance bot rolls every die, takes the first ware at the Bazaar, aims Lucky Dice at the Bazaar or a Sweet Well, and collects every lap prize. Its first run gave a die for one beast in four; it kills about 70 beasts an hour, so it rolled 957 times in 30 hours, travelled 153 laps and reached Rainwyrm Lv 20 two and a half hours early (1,181 minutes against about 1,337). With one beast in 20 and a quarter-crate (not one and a half) at the resource stops, two free-to-play runs reached Lv 20 at 1,329 and 1,348 minutes, unchanged, after about 60 laps (16 a season, past the lap 13 prize; the Legendary pouch at lap 20 stays a chase). The whale bot, with the daily pack, reached Lv 20 at 746 minutes (799 before).

**Why Growth Packs exist.** Before them, the whale bot finished the story at the same time as the $80 dolphin (about 9 hours) and reached Lv 20 no sooner. A crate grows with the Rainwyrm's level more slowly than building costs do: a late Rainwyrm level (the wyrm plus the six buildings it needs) costs about 45,000 Starglass in crates, so $1,000 of Starglass bought about two levels, and the whale bot spent much of it on recruits instead. Growth Packs are sized to the next level, so money keeps buying progress all the way to Lv 20. That is the spend ladder a high-grossing game in this genre needs. They also give every level-up a buying moment.

Run-to-run spread is wide (gacha luck and raid timing move stage 60 and stage 100 by an hour or more), so tune with several seeds, not one.

In every run the keep never ran dry (zero minutes thirsty or dormant) and sickness stayed under 0.01% of survivor-time.

A human player is less efficient than the bot, so expect the story to take roughly two to three times as long: about 25-35 hours of play to the Ember Throne, 50-80 to wake the Mother of Rains, and longer still to tell every Hero Tale and forge the gear in full.

Other measured outcomes:

- **Water is the first lesson.** A new keep loses water every minute until the first quest (dig the Deep Well) is done, so players learn the core mechanic in the first minute.
- **Every resource is used.** Building costs draw on stone, water (mudbrick and mortar), food and copper from level 3 up. The Forge turns late-game stone into Sunsteel. Copper is the late-game constraint; food piles up for training and donations.
- **Nights matter.** Desert nights run 7°C colder and middays 2°C hotter, so a wyrm left on Steady wastes water after dark. Attuned mist drizzles at night and pours before storms. That alone saves enough water to speed the free player's story by about a tenth.
- **Keep life rewards attention.** An always-attentive player gets about 15% more output from surplus bubbles and 10-15% more water from showers. Incidents roughly break even, and merchants turn surplus food into journals, Rain Charms and the scarce resource.
- **Old saves carry over.** Saves from 2.0 and 2.1 load into 4.3 at the same chapter quest (quests added since are stepped over). A finished 2.x game continues straight into the Act II quests, and a save already in the Burning Line becomes Act II progress.
- **No errors** across all automated runs.
